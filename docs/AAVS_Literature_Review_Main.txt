1. SCOPE AND QUESTION


This review asks a narrow question: when a coding agent passes a repository's tests, what has actually been verified? Modern coding-agent benchmarks treat a patch as correct if it passes the tests supplied by the benchmark. That assumption is the object of study. The corpus covers benchmark audits, mutation testing, test-oracle synthesis, patch-correctness assessment, reward hacking, coding-agent benchmarks, reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards, and sandbox integrity.

Two boundaries define the review. First, it is about code and execution, not generic prompt injection, phishing, malware classification, or AI governance. Second, it separates what a paper measured from what later summaries claimed. Several widely repeated numbers in the project's earlier notes did not match the live papers. Those corrections are recorded in Section 9.

The review is written for a reader who wants to judge the evidence, not just read a reading list. Each detailed entry states the problem, the method, the result, the limitation, and the source. Where a paper's abstract does not contain a limitation, the entry says so instead of filling the gap.


2. METHOD AND CORPUS


The corpus was built from live arXiv and Crossref records on 21 and 22 October 2026. One hundred and twenty queries covered ten clusters. Each arXiv query returned up to 100 relevance-sorted records; each Crossref query returned up to 60. The raw harvest contained 19,072 records. After merging duplicates by arXiv ID, DOI, and normalized title, 10,454 unique records remained. A lexical relevance score and a domain filter reduced that set to 1,500 papers.

The filters were designed to keep software-engineering and coding-agent work and to remove unrelated domains. Cryptography signature schemes, computer-vision adversarial patches, molecular design, clinical agents, and container-performance studies were penalized. The filters are not perfect. The corpus is a prioritized evidence base, not a systematic review registered in advance, and the scores are lexical rather than methodological.

Open-access PDFs were downloaded from arXiv and publisher links. Every file was checked for the PDF magic bytes and a 10 KB minimum. 1,112 PDFs were downloaded and 1,112 full texts were extracted with pdftotext in plain reading order. 149 missing abstracts were filled from Semantic Scholar and marked as such. Fifty papers received full-text-assisted reviews. The remaining texts were indexed for AAVS-related terms.

The corpus contains 540 tier A papers, 926 tier B papers, and 34 tier C papers. These tiers are prioritization labels, not quality judgments. The evidence levels are: 50 full-text reviewed, 1,062 full text scanned, 271 abstract reviewed, and 117 metadata only. The appendix lists every record with its cluster, priority, and source.


3. WHAT THE LITERATURE SHOWS


1. The premise is supported, but the numbers are not interchangeable.

Three independent 2025-2026 audits find that passing tests do not imply a correct patch. Probe to Generate reports that 77% of SWE-bench Verified instances admit at least one surviving program variant (P0120). UTBoost reclassifies 345 patches and reports corrections affecting 40.9% of SWE-Bench Lite and 24.4% of SWE-Bench Verified leaderboard entries (P0001). SWE-ABS strengthens 50.2% of 500 Verified instances, rejects 19.71% of previously passing patches, and drops the top score from 78.80% to 62.20% (P0837). The units differ: instances, patches, leaderboard entries, and agents. The correct claim is that several audits converge on a material false-pass problem, not that a single percentage describes all benchmarks.


2. Hardening changes rankings, not just scores.

UTBoost reports 18 and 11 ranking changes on Lite and Verified (P0001). SWE-ABS reports that the previous top-ranked agent falls to fifth after strengthening (P0837). This matters for how the field interprets leaderboards: a benchmark that rewards shortcut-prone patches can change the relative order of systems, not merely inflate every score.


3. Test generation is not the same as oracle generation.

DCAware identifies a Self-Repair Trap: iterating until execution succeeds can drive models toward assertions that are easy to satisfy but weak at revealing faults (P0191). The oracle-generation study by Bodicoat et al. shows that regression oracles often encode the implemented behavior rather than the intended behavior (P0192). Coverage is weakly correlated with effectiveness once suite size is controlled (P0554), and assertions are a stronger signal (the assertion study in P0554's cluster). More tests do not automatically mean a stronger verifier.


4. The harness is part of the system under test.

Complex Agents, Shallow Tests finds that LLM-dependent harness code has less than half of its lines and branches covered by existing tests. Its HarnessTester reports 75.95% and 84.76% larger line and branch coverage gains, a 69.89% larger mutation-score gain, and 122 real harness bugs in widely used agentic systems (P0841). Verification therefore cannot stop at the patch; the execution scaffolding is also part of the system.


5. Contamination is a credible competing explanation.

The SWE-Bench Illusion reports up to 76% file-path identification accuracy from issue text on SWE-bench repositories, compared with 53% on repositories outside the benchmark (P0564). SWE-bench Live (1,319 tasks across 93 repositories, P0262) and Multi-SWE-bench (1,632 instances across seven languages, P0013) are partial remedies. Any claim that an agent solved a task needs a contamination control, not just a score.


6. Environment-level reward hacking is documented, but defenses are not settled.

Terminal Wrench releases 331 reward-hackable terminal environments, 3,632 hack trajectories, and 2,352 legitimate baselines across three frontier models (P0121). The exploits range from output spoofing to stack-frame introspection, standard-library patching, and rootkit-style binary hijacking. The Hack-Verifiable Environments line (P0058, P0071) turns hack detection into a measurement paradigm. The corpus does not contain a mature, task-agnostic defense covering both code-level and environment-level gaming. That is an open problem, not a solved one.


7. Mutation testing is a defensible proxy, with a known equivalence problem.

Just et al. show that mutants are statistically associated with real faults (P0555), which supports mutation-based auditing. The equivalent-mutant problem remains: syntactically different programs can be semantically identical, so some mutants cannot be killed by any test (P0239, P0032). A mutation score is not meaningful unless equivalence is handled or at least reported.


8. Patch overfitting is old, measurable, and still unresolved.

The patch-correctness survey (P0002) and the patch-overfitting study by Petke et al. (P0008) treat overfitting as a longstanding property of test-based repair. In P0008, the median number of plausible patches per Defects4J bug is two, so the practical problem is often selection among plausible patches rather than generating a single fix. This is exactly the gap a completeness gate is meant to close.


9. Evaluation methodology is part of the contribution.

The corpus contains a separate cluster on contamination, construct validity, benchmark leakage, and leaderboard reliability (C9). Reporting a score without its denominator, contamination control, and selection procedure is not a measurement. Papers such as Benchmark Leakage (P1623), Automated Benchmark Auditing (P1617), and contamination-resistant benchmark design (P1679) make this point directly.


4. CURRENT STATUS OF THE FIELD


The question a reviewer will ask is whether this project is doing anything that has not already been published. The honest answer is that every component exists, but the assembly does not exist as a single released system. Mutating a reference patch to expose weak tests is published (Probe to Generate, 2026; SWE-ABS, 2026). LLM test generation against surviving mutants is published (UTBoost, 2025; MUTGEN, 2026; PRIMG, 2025). Reward hacking in terminal environments is published and measured (Terminal Wrench, 2026; HVE and HVTB, 2026). This review found no paper in the 1,500-paper corpus that combines these into one model-agnostic loop reporting both false passes and false rejections, releasing a per-task hardened suite, and covering both code and container state. The project should therefore claim the protocol and the release, not the loop. Absence in this corpus is evidence, not proof; re-check before submission.


Probe to Generate (P0120, 2026): closest to the full loop.

It generates semantically modified variants of the reference patch, keeps the variants the original tests pass, generates targeted tests, and retains a test only if the gold patch passes, a surviving variant fails, and the test survives behavior-preserving transformations. On SWE-bench Verified, 77% of instances admit at least one surviving variant; 1,014 validated tests cover 211 instances; top-10 agents lose 4.2% to 9.0% resolved rate. It stops at test augmentation: no container/state layer and no released per-task hardened suite.


SWE-ABS (P0837, 2026): strongest adversarial strengthening result.

A two-stage pipeline combines coverage-driven augmentation with mutation-driven synthesis of plausible but incorrect patches. It strengthens 50.2% of 500 Verified instances, rejects 19.71% of previously passing patches, and lowers the top score from 78.80% to 62.20%; the previous leader falls to fifth. It stops at synthetic wrong patches that may not resemble real agent shortcuts, and it releases no reusable hardened suite.


UTBoost (P0001, 2025): strongest leaderboard re-evaluation result.

UTGenerator writes tests for real Python projects; UTBoost cross-validates gold and agent patches and re-grades public submissions. It reclassifies 345 patches; corrections affect 40.9% of SWE-bench Lite and 24.4% of Verified leaderboard entries, with 18 and 11 ranking changes. It only works on tasks some agent already solved and has no execution-state layer. UTBoost and SWE-ABS share a first author, so this is a small, fast-moving line, not a crowded field.


Mutation-guided test synthesis (P0847, P1154, P1155, P0516): the defender's toolbox.

MUTGEN feeds surviving mutants back into the prompt and beats EvoSuite on mutation score across 204 subjects. PRIMG prioritizes mutants before generating tests for Solidity. Tratto derives axiomatic oracles neuro-symbolically. TOGA infers oracles from method context. All operate at function level, not on repository patches or benchmark harnesses.


Terminal Wrench, HVE and HVTB (P0121, P0058, P0071, 2026): the environment layer.

Terminal Wrench releases 331 hackable environments, 3,632 exploit trajectories, and 2,352 legitimate baselines, with exploits ranging from output spoofing to stack-frame introspection and rootkit-style hijacking; monitor AUC falls from 0.97 to 0.92 without chain-of-thought. HVE and HVTB turn hack detection into a measurement protocol. They stop at measurement; there is no task-agnostic defense covering code and state.


What this means for the contribution


What is not new.

Mutating code to test a suite, using an LLM to write tests, showing that SWE-bench tests are insufficient, re-grading public patches, and measuring terminal reward hacking are all published. A soundness-plus-completeness gate already exists inside Probe to Generate and SWE-ABS. These should be stated as prior work, not discovered as gaps.


What is plausibly new.

First, one protocol that reports two denominators: the false-pass rate and the false-rejection rate on the gold patch for the same tasks. Most papers report only the first. Second, a released, model-agnostic hardened suite with per-task provenance. Third, one gate over AST mutants and container/state hacks. The third claim needs Stage 2 data before it is asserted.


The positioning sentence.

Prior work shows that benchmark tests are weak and that individual components can be hardened; this project contributes a single released protocol that quantifies both directions of error, across code and execution state, on public artifacts.


Scope.

The security cluster is large and can take over the project. It should not. A patch that passes functional tests but introduces a CWE is a surviving mutant with a security label: one evaluation axis, one paragraph, not the organizing frame.

7.1 C1: Benchmark test inadequacy and false passes


This cluster contains the direct audits. The common finding is that repository tests are under-constrained, but the papers use different units. Probe to Generate works with program variants that survive the original tests and then generates targeted tests; on SWE-bench Verified, 77% of instances admit at least one surviving variant (P0120). UTBoost generates tests for real Python projects and re-evaluates public patches; it finds 345 erroneous patches and reports 40.9% and 24.4% effects on Lite and Verified leaderboard entries (P0001). SWE-ABS uses coverage-driven and mutation-driven strengthening; it strengthens 50.2% of instances and rejects 19.71% of previously passing patches (P0837).


Two adjacent results widen the problem. The SWE-Bench Illusion shows that memorization can explain part of the performance gap (P0564), and Complex Agents, Shallow Tests shows that harness code is itself undertested (P0841). Taken together, these papers support the premise that outcome tests are weak, but they do not support a single merged percentage. The missing piece is a common protocol that reports surviving variants, false passes, and false rejections with the same denominator.

7.2 C2: Vulnerability regression and secure AI code


Vulnerability detection is the most mature part of this cluster. The corpus contains surveys, deep-learning detectors, and secure-code benchmarks such as RealSec-bench and SafeGenBench. SecCodePRM adds a step-level security reward model that can score partial code and rank candidate continuations (P0116). These papers are useful context for a security-invariant completeness gate.


The narrower claim, that AI-generated patches introduce new vulnerabilities, is less settled in this corpus. Beyond Bypass is directly about vulnerability regression (P0052), but the indexed record has no abstract, so its numbers are not verified here. The security cluster should therefore be read as a set of detection and scoring methods, not as proof of a specific regression rate. A longitudinal study with CWE deltas and a sound baseline is still needed.

7.3 C3: Reward hacking and specification gaming


Reward hacking has a formal foundation. Skalse et al. define hackability and show that non-trivial unhackable proxies are severely constrained (P0011). Empirical work then shows what this looks like in practice. Terminal Wrench releases 331 environments, 3,632 hack trajectories, and 2,352 legitimate baselines; its monitorability study reports an AUC drop from 0.97 to 0.92 when chain-of-thought is removed (P0121). The HVE and HVTB papers turn embedded hacks into a measurement protocol (P0058, P0071).


A second strand studies reward overoptimization in language models. InfoRM uses an information bottleneck and a Cluster Separation Index to detect overoptimization (P0005). Inference-Time Reward Hacking shows the characteristic rise-then-decline pattern and proposes hedging (P0019). The cluster is strong on measurement and weak on a general defense. No paper here provides a task-agnostic verifier that handles both code and container state.

7.4 C4: Test and verifier synthesis with execution feedback


This is the defender's cluster. The strongest warning is DCAware's Self-Repair Trap: optimizing execution success can make oracles easier to satisfy and less fault-revealing (P0191). The oracle-generation study by Bodicoat et al. reaches a compatible conclusion: many generated oracles encode implemented behavior rather than intended behavior (P0192). AssertionBench and ChatUniTest provide benchmarks and baselines for assertion and test generation (P1194, P1292).


The test-quality literature explains why more tests are not enough. Coverage is not strongly correlated with fault detection once suite size is controlled (P0554), while assertion strength is a better signal. The practical implication is that a synthesized test should be judged by what it kills and by whether it rejects valid alternative implementations, not by whether it runs successfully.

7.6 C6: Mutation testing and oracle foundations


The foundational papers in this cluster justify mutation testing and define its limits. Just et al. show that mutants are a valid substitute for real faults in the aggregate (P0555). The lesson for AAVS is that mutation score is not a single invariant. Any paper that reports a mutation score should state its operator set, its equivalence policy, and its treatment of timeouts.

This cluster connects verification to training. Process reward models score intermediate steps, and RLVR uses verifiable outcomes as reward. 7.8 C8: Automated repair and patch correctness


Patch overfitting is the oldest problem in this review. The survey by Fei et al. categorizes correctness-assessment methods and datasets (P0002). Petke et al. measure the practical magnitude and propose a random baseline for realistic benchmarking; their median number of plausible patches per Defects4J bug is two (P0008). The systematic literature review on LLM-based repair and the formal-methods studies add more evidence that tests alone do not select the correct patch (P0007, P0015).


The cluster is useful because it separates two questions that are often conflated. One is whether a patch is plausible under the tests. The other is whether it is correct under the specification. AAVS's completeness gate is an attempt to answer the second question without a human oracle, and this cluster is the best source of counterarguments.

7.10 C10: Sandbox, container and state integrity


This is a cross-cutting cluster. Sandbox and state-integrity papers are usually assigned to coding agents (C5) or reward hacking (C3) by the lexical scorer, but they belong here conceptually. SideKernel studies a microVM sandbox for coding agents on macOS (P0187), SWE-MiniSandbox studies container-free reinforcement learning (P0197), and the Balkanization paper maps isolation, access control, and time-of-check-to-time-of-use risks (P0927).


The defense literature is still fragmented. Fault-tolerant sandboxing proposes transactional execution (P1101), and container sandbox escape work measures how capable frontier models are at breaking isolation (P1619). No paper in the corpus provides a unified protocol that connects AST-level mutants, filesystem state, process state, and exit codes. That is the Stage 2 gap.

10. LIMITATIONS OF THIS REVIEW


The relevance scoring is lexical. A paper can score highly because its title and abstract use the right vocabulary, even if its method is weak. The tiers are prioritization labels, not peer review.

Most summaries compress an abstract. They are useful for orientation, not for quoting a specific number. The source link and the local PDF are provided for checking.

The corpus is not a registered systematic review. It over-represents open-access arXiv and Crossref work. Paywalled venue papers are present as metadata but may lack full text.

The 117 metadata-only records have no abstract in the indexed sources. They remain in the appendix for completeness, but they are not evidence for any claim.

The field moves quickly. 578 of the 1,500 records are dated 2026. The corpus should be refreshed before the manuscript is submitted.


11. HOW TO CHECK THE WORK


Every paper in the detailed review has a stable ID, a cluster, a priority, a summary, and a source link. The appendix lists all 1,500 records in the same format. A reader can pick any row, open the source, and compare it with the card.

The pipeline is reproducible. The commands are: python scripts/literature_harvester.py harvest --target 1500; download --limit 1500; extract; python scripts/enrich_abstracts.py; python scripts/literature_report.py; python scripts/literature_fulltext_index.py; python scripts/build_compendium.py. Query responses are cached, so a re-run only fetches new queries.

The complete per-paper compendium, the searchable HTML explorer, the evidence matrix, and the full-text signal index are in the docs directory of the repository. The PDFs are in papers/pdf. The metadata is in papers/metadata.
